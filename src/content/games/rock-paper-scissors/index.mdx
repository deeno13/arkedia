Rock Paper Scissors is useful because learners understand the rules almost instantly. That leaves more room to focus on what the game reveals: repeated outcomes, strategic habits, and the difference between a fair system and a predictable opponent.

## How the game works

Two players reveal one of three options at the same time: rock, paper, or scissors. Rock beats scissors, scissors beats paper, and paper beats rock. If both players choose the same option, the round is a draw.

The rules create a balanced cycle. No single move is strongest on its own, which makes the game a clear introduction to fairness and mixed strategies.

## Example

If a class records thirty rounds and notices that one player chooses rock far more often than the others, the game shifts from pure randomness to pattern reading. Students can start asking whether the best response should depend on probability alone or on observed behavior.

## Strategy tips

- treat one round as a single outcome, but several rounds as a data set
- watch for habits in repeated play rather than assuming every round is random
- remember that a fair ruleset can still produce predictable players
- use the result history to talk about evidence, not superstition

## Educational value

This game gives teachers a low-friction way to discuss probability, fairness, expected outcomes, and human prediction. It is especially useful because the rules are easy enough that the conversation can move quickly from “what happened” to “why it happened.”

<Callout title="Teaching prompt">
  Have learners predict the next move after watching a short series of rounds, then compare whether they relied on data, assumptions, or a story they invented after the fact.
</Callout>

## Related concepts

Rock Paper Scissors connects to probability, game theory, repeated experiments, and pattern spotting. It also works well as a first candidate for a playable widget because the rules are tiny and the feedback is immediate.
